Section 1

Why hybridisation is needed

Hybridisation is a model within valence bond theory that explains why atoms form bonds in specific directions and at specific angles. It is particularly useful when we need to connect the chemical formula, the orbitals and the three-dimensional shape of a molecule.

The “problem” of methane

In its ground state, carbon (6C: 1s22s22p2) has two unpaired electrons. We would therefore expect it to form two bonds at an angle of 90°. Experimentally, however, in methane, CH4:

  • carbon forms four C–H bonds;
  • the four bonds are equivalent (same length, same energy);
  • the molecule is tetrahedral, with angles of 109.5°.

Step 1 — Promotion. One 2s electron is promoted to the empty 2p orbital, and carbon acquires four unpaired electrons. The energy needed for promotion is recovered because more bonds are formed.

Step 2 — Hybridisation. If carbon used its 2s and 2p orbitals as they are, one bond would differ from the other three. The orbitals mix and give equivalent hybrid orbitals.

Definition

Hybridisation is the linear combination (mixing) of atomic orbitals of the same atom, which produces new, equivalent atomic orbitals: the hybrid orbitals.

Properties of hybrid orbitals

  • Their number equals the number of atomic orbitals that are mixed.
  • They have the same shape and the same energy, intermediate between s and p, and differ only in orientation.
  • They are oriented so as to be as far apart as possible.
  • Hybrid orbitals form only σ bonds; π bonds are formed by p orbitals that have not been hybridised.

Remember

Hybridisation is a model, not something that we “see”. Orbitals are not visible objects; hybridisation is a concise way of predicting and explaining geometry and bonding.

Research in chemistry education shows that hybridisation is a demanding concept, because it combines abstract ideas about orbitals with geometry and spatial reasoning (Çalış, 2018; Petritis et al., 2022; Salame et al., 2022).
